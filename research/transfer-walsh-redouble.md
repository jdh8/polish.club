# What XX means when 1♣ (X) 1♦ shows hearts

Checked 9 November 2026. The question: among systems where responder's 1♦
over a takeout double of 1♣ shows hearts, what does responder's redouble
mean? The evidence sections record precedents only; the last section is
analysis. Nothing here edits the book.

Inclusion needs a source establishing 1♦ = hearts directly after 1♣ (X).
Uncontested Transfer Walsh is not enough, and neither is transfers over
1♦/1M openings or over overcalls only. A statement covering all one-level
openings counts. The 1♣ labels are **small** (multi-way with a weak balanced
range), **strong** (artificial 15+) and **natural** (including 2+ and
nebulous clubs). Ranges are quoted as printed.

**Conclusion 1.** XX is mostly values or diamonds. Of 83 sources that define
XX, 44 play it as values (usually 9+ or 10+, often penalty-oriented) and 22 as
the first step of the transfer ladder, showing diamonds. The remaining 17
split among balanced values, clubs, other meanings and two strong-club
negatives. A further 34 confirm 1♦ = hearts but say nothing usable about XX.

**Conclusion 2.** No played system was found with both 1♦ = hearts and XX as
the weakest hand. The nearest is [mycroft's Precision][mycroft], where XX is
a 5–7 negative but pass is weaker at 0–4. [Bluthman][bluthman] proposes
splitting pass and XX into 0–3 and 4–7, in either order, without saying
anyone plays it; 0–3 on XX is the only weakest-hand reading. So the book's
combination of XX = 0–4, P = forcing 5+ and 1♦ = hearts has no precedent in
these sources.

**Conclusion 3.** The two exact matches for a negative XX found earlier,
[RepeL][repel] and [Toad Club][toad], do not play 1♦ = hearts. RepeL's 1♦ is
6–8 unbalanced with no 4-card major or both majors (1♥ shows hearts). Toad's 1♦ is a
game-forcing club transfer. Negative XX and a heart-showing 1♦ have so far
appeared separately.

**Conclusion 4.** Among Polish-style clubs that write XX down, values
dominates: 12 of 17 small-club sources that define XX, such as Bizon–Blat
(“xx=10+ without fit”) and Gotard–Omernik. [Sublime][sublime] and a personal
[WJ XXI adaptation][wjxxi] use XX for diamonds and keep pass as the weak call.
Efraimsson–Morath also play XX as diamonds, and so does strong-club
[Totolotek][totolotek] (0–9 with 5+♦, pass 0–7 or 8+ roughly balanced).
Sweden's Gullberg–Morath is the outlier, with XX as both minors. Pairs from
the Bocheński team print “pass – negative” with no XX meaning.

Counts are of sources found, not of how widely a method is played. Cards from
one team or country cluster, and the Polish, Australian and European
tournament collections were the ones that could be scanned.

Marks used in the tables:

- ‡ 1♦ = hearts is inferred from a transfer ladder or a generic statement
  rather than spelled out for 1♦ over the double.
- † Found and checked by a single reader in the final gap search; the other
  entries were also re-read by a second, skeptical reader.
- (low) The source is terse or forum-only, so treat the entry as weak.

## XX = diamonds

XX begins the ladder: XX → ♦, 1♦ → ♥, 1♥ → ♠. Many cards state only “transfers
from XX”, so the suit is inferred from the step. This typically frees 1♠ for
no-major or club hands.

## Analysis

**The book's XX is a real departure inside this group.** The book gives up
the penalty-oriented or diamond-showing XX that most of these sources use. In
return it gets a low-level brake on a strong opener and keeps pass for the 5+
hands. The sources offer no test of that trade.

**The diamond slot is the structural cost.** The 22 XX = diamonds sources
start the ladder at XX, which leaves 1♠ for other hands. Because the book spends XX on
the negative, diamonds must go to 1♠ and clubs to 1NT. Precedents for
diamonds on 1♠ exist: Ravera (“1S shows diamonds”) and hybrids that make 1♠
“weak balanced or any diamond hand” (Hargreaves–McOrmond, Mikkelsen–Furunes
with “no M or 4+♦”). None is paired with a negative XX.

**No source here makes pass forcing.** Where pass is defined it is weak
(WJ XXI adaptation, Bocheński pairs, Sublime), weak with a strong-hand option
(Spiljak–Rase, Petrov–Bauer, Goose), or a split range (Totolotek). The book
inverts that. Citing strong-club practice for the
inversion is defensible only for clubs without a heart-showing 1♦ (see
RepeL), and the current citation of Viking Precision 2007 at
[Competition.md](../src/1C/Competition.md) is not supported by that file.

Whether the inversion pays is a simulation question. Nothing surveyed here
measures it.
